\chapter{Introduction}
"All models are wrong, but some are useful." is a famous quote by the statistician George E. P. Box. This quote captures a fundamental truth of empirical modelling: no model can perfectly describe empirical phenomena, but models can still provide valuable insights and predictions. Yield-curve forecasting is a prime example of this principle. Although no existing model has been able to perfectly represent and predict the term structure of interest rates with complete precision, models that generate reasonably accurate forecasts can still be extremely useful and provide valuable economic benefits in financial markets \parencite[p. 2]{CaldeiraMouraSantos2016}.

The yield curve represents the relationship between interest rates and the maturities of debt securities issued by the same entity. The overall shape of the curve conveys important information about market expectations regarding future interest rates, inflation, and economic growth. This makes the forecasting of yield curves a critical task of immense interest to bond market participants, risk managers, policymakers and even equity and commodity investors \parencite[p. 3]{das2025yieldcurve}. For reference, the U.S. bond market was valued at approximately \$51~trillion\footnote{SIFMA (2025) \url{https://www.sifma.org/resources/research/statistics/fact-book/}.} in 2025, almost as large as the U.S. equity market at \$58~trillion\footnote{Siblis Research (2025)\url{https://siblisresearch.com/data/us-stock-market-value/}.}.

Over the past decades, the modelling and forecasting of yield curves have been dominated by classical econometric approaches. A particularly influential framework is the Nelson--Siegel model \parencite{nelson1987parsimonious}, which represents the yield curve as a function of three latent factors. Building on this, \textcite{diebold2006forecasting} proposed a dynamic version of the Nelson--Siegel model where these factors evolve over time according to simple autoregressive processes. Due to its remarkable effectiveness for forecasting yield curves, the Diebold-Li model has become the a widely used benchmark in yield-curve forecasting \parencite[pp. 1-2]{swanson2018predicting}.

While these model remain the dominant benchmark, their linear and time-invariant structure limits performance in environments with nonlinear and high-dimensional nature \parencite[p. 2]{Wang2025}. In response, recent research has explored machine-learning models as more flexible approaches to yield-curve forecasting. Techniques such as Ridge Regression \parencite{hoerl1970ridge} and Gradient Boosting \parencite{chen2016xgboost} can capture complex relationships in the data and may offer improved predictive accuracy in volatile or changing market regimes as observed in today's financial markets \parencite[p. 2]{Wang2025}.

Against this background, the central objective of this thesis is to assess whether selected machine-learning models can deliver systematically better yield-curve forecasts than classical econometric approaches when both are trained on the same information set. The first research question examines to what extent ridge regression and gradient boosting improve statistical forecast accuracy across maturities and horizons relative to established benchmarks such as the Dynamic-Nelson-Siegel model and the random walk. The second research question investigates whether any improvement in predictive accuracy translates into economic value, measured through the profitability of  a simple trading strategy and the risk reduction in a portfolio optimization problem. By jointly evaluating statistical performance and economic outcomes, the thesis aims to determine whether the additional modelling complexity of machine-learning methods is justified in practice.

The thesis is structured as follows: in Chapter 2, an overview of related literature and relevant prior work is provided. A detailed descripted of the used data is presented in Chapter 3. Chapter 4 describes the Methodology, covering the different econometrical and machine-learning models as well as the statistical and economical evaluation design. Chapter 5 presents the results of the empirical analysis, evaluating the performance of the different models. In Chapter 6, a robustness check in form of a subsample analysis is conducted. Finally, in Chapter 7 the results of this thesis are discussed and conclusions are drawn.

\chapter{Related Literature}
Early work on term-structure modelling dates back to \textcite{durand1942basic}, who first estimated smooth yield-curves using polynomial functions. A major breakthrough came with the introduction of the Nelson--Siegel model \parencite{nelson1987parsimonious}, which represents the yield-curve using three latent factors interpreted as level, slope and curvature. \textcite{diebold2006forecasting} extended this framework into a dynamic model by modelling the factors with autoregressive processes, establishing the Dynamic Nelson--Siegel (DNS) model. Various extensions have been proposed to improve the DNS model, including the incorporation of macroeconomics variables or regime dynamics \parencite{diebold2006macroeconomy}, yet the DNS model remains the dominant benchmark for yield-curve forecasting due to its simplicity, interpretability and strong empirical performance \parencite[pp. 1-2]{swanson2018predicting}.

Complementing these econometric models, a growing amount of literature applies machine-learning methods to yield-curve forecasting. \textcite{sambasivan2017statistical} demonstrate that Gaussian process regression delivers competitive accuracy, particularly for medium- and long-term maturities. In a comprehensive comparison of artificial neural networks, \textcite{nunes2019comparison} show that multilayer perceptrons outperform alternative machine-learning approaches. \textcite{rahimi2020forecast} document that random forests provide strong predictive performance at the short end of the curve. More recently, regime-switching models based on machine-learning classifiers have been shown to extract valuable state-dependent information for yield-curve prediction \parencite{bie2024machine}. These examples illustrate the fast growing research interest in applying machine-learning techniques to yield-curve forecasting.

In summary, prior research provided strong evidence that both econometric and machine-learning models can extract useful information for yield-curve forecasting. However, existing studies differ substantially in terms of data, sample periods, forecasting horizons, and evaluation, making a direct comparision across methods difficult. This paper adds to the existing literature in two important aspects. First, by providing a consistent and reproducable forecasting framework using daily U.S. Treasury yields from 2009-2025 and evaluating both econometric and machine-learning models. Second, by not only assessing the statistical accuracy but also the economic value through trading and risk-reduction simulations.

\section{Descriptive Statistics}

The empirical analysis in this thesis uses daily U.S. Treasury constant-maturity yields from January 2, 2009 to November 25, 2025. Specifically, the 1-, 2-, 5-, and 10-year maturities (DGS1, DGS2, DGS5, DGS10) are obtained from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED)\footnote{\url{https://fred.stlouisfed.org/}}. In total, the dataset contains 16.908 observations (4.227 per maturity). Given the short horizons of our forecasting task and following \textcite{nunes2019comparison} and \textcite{das2025yieldcurve} daily constant-maturity yields are chosen rather than the otherwise often chosen monthly constant-maturity yields. The sample begins in 2009 to focus on the post-crisis monetary regime and exlcude extreme distortions of the 2008 financial crisis. The dataset provides a clean and high-quality basis for comparing econometric and machine-learning models for yield-curve forecasting. A plot and descriptive statistics of the yield data can be found in Table 3.1 and Figure 3.1. Throughout this thesis, the terms yield, interest rate, and Treasury yield are used interchangeably to refer to continuously observed constant-maturity U.S. Treasury rates, while yield curve and term structure denote the cross-sectional relationship between yields and maturities at a given point in time.

Based on the plot and summary statistics, several key characteristics of the yield curve can be observed. First, yields exhibit an upward-sloping structure, with longer-maturity bonds offering higher yields than shorter-maturity bonds on average. Second, longer-maturity yields tend to be less volatile than shorter-maturity yields, as indicated by the decreasing standard deviations reported in Table~3.1. This is consistent with the stylized facts about the yield curve as described by \textcite[p. 343]{diebold2006forecasting}.

\section{Macroeconomic context}

The sample period spans several specific monetary policy regimes that are relevant for the interpretation of the forecasting results. Following the 2008 global financial crisis, U.S. Treasury yields remained low throughout much of the 2010s. During this period volatility was relatively low and yield movements were smooth. Between 2016 and 2019, the Federal Reserve gradually increased interest rates, leading to a modest upward trend in yields. In early 2020, yields collapsed abruptly across the entire maturity spectrum as the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic caused a severe economic shock and an aggressive policy response. Rapid rate cuts and large-scale asset purchases by the Federal Reserve pushed yields to historic lows at the beginning of 2021. Starting in 2022, U.S. Treasury yields increased sharply following restrictive monetary policy actions to fight high inflation. This period was characterized by visibly high volatility, yield curve inversions and significant uncertainty. The existence of these distinct regimes within the data set provides a robust environment to evaluate the performance of our forecasting models across different market conditions.

\chapter{Methodology}

Then, the econometric benchmark models, namely the Random Walk and Dynamic Nelson--Siegel model, are presented, including a brief description of their specification. \section{Forecasting Task}
The thesis is structured around a common forecasting task, concerning the U.S. Treasury yields at different maturities and horizons. Let $y_{t}^{(\tau)}$ denote the yield at time $t$ with maturity $\tau \in \{2,5,10\}$. The 1-year maturity rate is solely used as the risk-free rate in the analysis and is therefore no forecasting target. For each model we generate out-of-sample forecasts at horizons $h \in \{1,5,10,30\}$ business days ahead. Formally, at each time $t$ the goal is to produce a forecast
\[
\hat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t+h\mid t} = \mathcal{F}_{t}^{(\tau)}(h),
\]
where the notation $t+h \mid t$ denotes a forecast made at time $t$ for the yield at time $t+h$. $\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(\tau)}(h)$ denotes the model specific forecast operator that is based on the information set $\mathcal{F}_{t}$ available at time $t$.

While the RW and DNS models directly forecast yield levels, the machine-learning models forecast yield changes over the prediction horizon and then reconstruct the yield level using the last observed yield.
\[
\hat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t+h\mid t} = y^{(\tau)}_{t} + \widehat{\Delta y}^{(\tau)}_{t+h\mid t}.
\]
This transformation is motivated by standard time-series theory that recommends focusing on differences of persistent series to obtain more stable forecasts \parencite[Chapter 15]{hamilton1994}.

Moving along the OOS period, we employ an expanding window forecasting scheme because recent work on yield curve forecasting finds that ensemble methods like XGBoost achieve a better performance using this approach \parencite[p.~5]{das2025yieldcurve}.
The expanding window uses a fixed initial training sample from 2 January 2009 to 31 December 2018, which provides $2.501$ daily observations per maturity. Out-of-sample forecasts are then generated from 2 January 2019 to 25 November 2025, which results in $1.726$ daily observations per maturity in the evaluation period. At each forecast origin $t$ in the test sample the model is re-estimated on all observations from 2 January 2009 up to $t$, after which a forecast for $t+h$ is produced.

\section{Econometric Models}
This section introduces the econometric benchmark models used for forecasting the yield 
curve. First, the Random Walk model is presented as a minimal-assumption benchmark commonly used 
in the yield-curve literature. Then the Dynamic Nelson--Siegel model is described, 
including its specification and economic interpretation.
\subsection{Random Walk}
The random walk (RW) model serves as the simplest and most widely used benchmark in the yield curve forecasting literature. It assumes that the best forecast of a future yield is the most recent observed yield. Let $y^{(\tau)}_{t}$ denote the yield at time $t$ for maturity $\tau \in \{2,5,10\}$. The RW model produces an $h$-step-ahead forecast according to
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t+h \mid t} = y^{(\tau)}_{t}
\end{equation}

The RW model implies that yields follow a martingale difference process, meaning that future changes in yields are unpredictable given all information available at time $t$. Formally,
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}\left( y^{(\tau)}_{t+1} - y^{(\tau)}_{t} \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_{t} \right) = 0,
\end{equation*}
Thus the model contains no parameters and requires no estimation.

Despite its simplicity, the RW model remains a challenging benchmark for forecasting interest rates. Empirical studies such as \textcite[p. 2035]{CarrieroKapetaniosMarcellino2012} show that only very few models outperform the RW in terms of forecast accuracy. \textcite[p. 1]{swanson2018predicting} further document that after 2010 only a small number of forecasting models consistently "beat" a RW benchmark. The RW therefore provides a solid baseline against which the incremental value of more complex models can be evaluated.


\subsection{Dynamic Nelson--Siegel}
The Dynamic Nelson--Siegel (DNS) model combines the cross-sectional yield-curve representation of \textcite{nelson1987parsimonious} with the dynamic factor structure introduced by \textcite{diebold2006forecasting}. Let $y_{t}(\tau)$ denote the U.S. Treasury yield at time $t$ and maturity $\tau$. The Nelson–Siegel curve describes the shape of the yield curve at each point in time as
\begin{equation}
y_{t}^{(\tau)}
= \beta_{1,t}
+ \beta_{2,t} \left( \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda_{t} \tau}}{\lambda_{t} \tau} \right)
+ \beta_{3,t} \left( \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda_{t} \tau}}{\lambda_{t} \tau} - e^{-\lambda_{t} \tau} \right),
\label{eq:ns_curve}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{1,t}$, $\beta_{2,t}$, and $\beta_{3,t}$ are time-varying factors and $\lambda_{t}>0$ controls how quickly the factor loadings decline with maturity.

The three DNS factors have a clear economic interpretation \parencite[pp. 340-343]{diebold2006forecasting}. The first factor $\beta_{1,t}$ affects all maturities equally and reflects the general level of interest rates. The second factor $\beta_{2,t}$ loads more strongly on short maturities and captures the slope of the yield curve. The third factor $\beta_{3,t}$ loads on medium maturities and captures curvature. These three features summarise almost all of the shape variation typically observed in government bond markets such as upward sloping, downward sloping, humped, and S-shaped \parencite[p. 347]{diebold2006forecasting}. Because of this parsimony the DNS model is the dominant method for yield-curve forecasting at many central banks \parencite[p. 5]{bis2005zerocoupon}.

Following \textcite{diebold2006forecasting} we treat the factors as latent variables that evolve smoothly over time. Let
\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\beta_{1,t} \\
\beta_{2,t} \\
\beta_{3,t}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The factors follow a first-order vector autoregression,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}
= \boldsymbol{\mu} + A \boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_{t},
\label{eq:dns_var}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is a vector of intercepts, $A$ is a $3 \times 3$ coefficient matrix and $\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}$ is a disturbance term. This specification captures the persistence commonly observed in interest-rate movements \parencite[p. 343-347]{diebold2006forecasting}.

For each day $t$ we estimate the factors $\beta_{1,t}$, $\beta_{2,t}$ and $\beta_{3,t}$ by fitting equation~\eqref{eq:ns_curve} to the observed yields using ordinary least squares. This yields a time series of estimated factor vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}$, from which the VAR system in equation~\eqref{eq:dns_var} is estimated. The estimated VAR then produces $h$-step-ahead forecasts of the factors,
\begin{equation}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+h \mid t}
= \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} + \hat{A} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+h-1 \mid t},
\end{equation}
using the current factor estimates as a starting point. The forecasted factors are then translated into yield forecasts through the Nelson--Siegel model in equation~\eqref{eq:ns_curve}.

A key choice concerns the parameter $\lambda_{t}$, which determines where the curvature factor peaks. Following \textcite[p.~347]{diebold2006forecasting}'s recommendation we set $\lambda_{t}=0.0609$ for all $t$, which places the curvature peak at roughly 30 months.

\section{Machine-Learning Models}
In this section the machine-learning models used for yield-curve forecasting are described. 
First, the feature selection and the construction of the input information set are outlined. 
Then the two forecasting methods, Ridge Regression and Gradient Boosted Trees, 
are presented, including model specification, regularisation and their application in the forecasting task.

\subsection{Input Features}

The machine-learning models rely exclusively on information contained in the yield curve itself.
For each forecast date $t$, the input features consist of lagged observations of yield levels as well as simple transformations that summarize the cross-sectional shape of the yield curve.

Let $\{\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_M\}$ denote the set of maturities available in the dataset.
For a given lag length $p$, the baseline feature vector includes the most recent $p$ daily observations of each maturity,
\[
\mathbf{x}_t^{\text{levels}} = \left(
y_{t}^{(\tau_1)}, y_{t-1}^{(\tau_1)}, \ldots, y_{t-p+1}^{(\tau_1)},\;
\ldots,\;
y_{t}^{(\tau_M)}, y_{t-1}^{(\tau_M)}, \ldots, y_{t-p+1}^{(\tau_M)}
\right).
\]

In addition to yield levels, we augment the feature set with three linear combinations of yields that aim capture the shape of the yield curve. These transformations are designed to mimic \textcite[p. 341-342]{diebold2006forecasting}, while making use of our limited set of observed maturities.
Specifically, two slope measures and one curvature measure are included,
\[
\text{spread}_{5,2,t} = y_t^{(5)} - y_t^{(2)}, \qquad
\text{spread}_{10,2,t} = y_t^{(10)} - y_t^{(2)}, \qquad
\text{curv}_{5,t} = 2y_t^{(5)} - y_t^{(2)} - y_t^{(10)}.
\]

The final feature vector is constructed by stacking the lagged values of both yield levels and the derived slope and curvature measures,
\[
\mathbf{x}_t =
\bigl(
\mathbf{x}_t^{\text{levels}},\;
\text{spread}_{5,2,t}, \ldots, \text{spread}_{5,2,t-p+1},\;
\text{spread}_{10,2,t}, \ldots, \text{spread}_{10,2,t-p+1},\;
\text{curv}_{5,t}, \ldots, \text{curv}_{5,t-p+1}
\bigr).
\]

This representation allows the machine-learning models to exploit both the time-series dynamics of individual yields and economically meaningful summaries of the yield curve’s cross-sectional shape.

Although previous research has shown that incorporating macroeconomic variables can improve predictive accuracy \parencite[p.~16]{diebold2006macroeconomy}, such extensions would complicate the comparison with econometric benchmark models.
Therefore, the machine-learning models are restricted to yield-curve information only, ensuring a clean and transparent comparison across modeling approaches.


\subsection{Ridge Regression}

Ridge regression is a regularized extension of ordinary least squares (OLS) that is designed to improve predictive performance in settings with many potentially redundant or noisy predictors \parencite[pp.~55--56]{hoerl1970ridge}. This situation arises in yield-curve forecasting, where predictors are constructed from multiple maturities and their lags, which are known to share strong common variation driven by a small number of underlying factors \parencite{litterman1991common}.


Ordinary least squares estimates the coefficient vector $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ by minimising the sum of squared residuals,
\[
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}
= \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\bigl(
y_{t+h}^{(\tau)} - \mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
\bigr)^{2}.
\]

Ridge regression modifies this objective by adding a penalty term on the magnitude of the coefficient vector,

\begin{equation}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{ridge}}
= \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
\left[
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\bigl(
y_{t+h}^{(\tau)} - \mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
\bigr)^{2}
+ \alpha \lVert \boldsymbol{\beta} \rVert^{2}
\right],
\label{eq:ridge}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha \ge 0$ controls the strength of the regularisation. For $\alpha = 0$, the estimator reduces to OLS, while larger values of $\alpha$ increasingly shrink coefficients toward zero.
From an intuitive perspective, ridge regression discourages large and unstable coefficient estimates by penalizing large coefficient magnitudes.

Ridge regression was selected as one of the machine-learning models because it preserves the interpretability of linear forecasting approaches while providing a mechanism to control overfitting. Therefore, it acts as a good middle ground between the econometric models and the more complex nonlinear machine-learning method that follows.

\subsection{Gradient Boosted Trees}
Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a tree-based ensemble learning method widely used in regression and forecasting applications due to its strong predictive performance and flexibility \parencite{chen2016xgboost}. In contrast to linear models, XGBoost is capable of capturing nonlinear relationships and interaction effects among predictors because of its tree-based structure.

XGBoost builds on regression trees as base learners. A single regression tree partitions the predictor space into separate regions using a binary splitting rules of the form $x_j < c$. In each of the seperated regions, the model assigns a constant prediction to all observations. A simplified example of such a tree structure is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:regression_tree_example}. While individual trees are typically weak predictors, they can be combined to form a powerful forecasting model, a concept also knows as "wisdom of the crowd".

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{regression_tree_example.png}
    \caption[
    Example of a regression tree
    ]{
    Example of a regression tree.\\
    (Source: Own representation)
    }
    \label{fig:regression_tree_example}
\end{figure}

Instead of fitting just a single tree, XGBoost constructs an ensemble of trees in a sequential manner. Each new tree is trained to improve upon the residual errors of the existing model. Let $F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_t)$ denote the ensemble after $m-1$ trees. The update step can be written as
\[
F_m(\mathbf{x}_t) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_t) + \eta\, h_m(\mathbf{x}_t),
\]
where $h_m(\cdot)$ is the newly fitted regression tree and $\eta$ is a learning rate that controls the contribution of each tree to the final prediction.
From an intuitive perspective, XGBoost can be viewed as a method that corrects its own forecasting mistakes in every iteration.

XGBoost is included in this thesis for multiple reasons. First, it is one of the most used machine-learning models by practitioners \footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com/kaggle-survey-2022}}. Second, \textcite[p. 11]{Wang2025} proposed it as a promising area of research after finding strong results in yield-curve forecasting with ensemble-based methods. Lastly, its flexibility allows us to explicitly assess whether nonlinearities and interaction effects provide value for short-horizon U.S.\ yield curve forecasting. 


\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}

Both Ridge Regression and XGBoost require the selection of model-specific hyperparameters that control regularisation strength, model complexity, and learning behaviour. To determine suitable value for theses hyperparameter, we apply a traditional grid search approach as described in the evaluation of machine learning models literature \parencite[Chapter 4]{zheng2015evaluating}. To avoid data leakage, the search is conducted entirely within the training sample using an expanding-window cross-validation procedure. The training sample spans January 2009 to December 2018, while the validation window extends from January 2014 to December 2018.

For Ridge Regression, we tune the regularisation parameter $\alpha$ and the number of included lagged yield observations $p$. For XGBoost, the tuned hyperparameters are again the lag length $p$, learning rate $\eta$, maximum tree depth $d$, and the number of boosting iterations $T$. For each maturity and horizon, we select the combination of hyperparameters that yields the lowest RMSE in the validation period. Finally, the selected hyperparameters are fixed, and the respective model is re-estimated on the full training sample before producing the final OOS forecasts.

\section{Forecast Accuracy Evaluation}

In this section, the accuracy evaluation metrics used to compare the forecasting performance of the different models are presented. First, the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is defined as the standard measure of statistical forecast accuracy. Then, the Diebold--Mariano test for comparing the predictive accuracy of competing forecasting models is introduced.

\subsection{Root-Mean-Squared-Error}

To measure the statistical accuracy of the yield forecasts, we use the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) which is one of the most-widely used metrics in the literature and was also used by \textcite[p. 356]{diebold2006forecasting}, \textcite[p. 28]{rahimi2020forecast} or \parencite[pp. 12-13]{CaldeiraMouraSantos2016}. RMSE summarizes how far the predictions deviate from the actual market yields on average. Through the squaring of the deviattions, large forecast mistakes are penalized more strongly. This makes RMSE well suited for financial applications, where large errors can result in substantial pricing or hedging impacts.

For a given maturity $\tau$ and forecast horizon $h$, the forecast error of a model at time $t$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
e^{(\tau)}_{t+h} = y^{(\tau)}_{t+h} - \widehat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t+h|t},
\end{equation}

The RMSE over $N$ forecasts is calculated as
\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE}^{(\tau)}_{h} =
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{t = T_0}^{T_1-h}
\left(e^{(\tau)}_{t+h}\right)^2
}.
\end{equation}

Lower RMSE values indicate more accurate forecasts. RMSE is reported separately by maturity and forecast horizon to compare model performance across the yield curve and over different time horizons.

\subsection{Diebold-Mariano Test}

To formally test whether two models have the same predictive accuracy, we apply the Diebold--Mariano test as introduced by \textcite{DieboldMariano1995}. The test compares the squared forecast errors of two models through the loss differential
\begin{equation}
d_{t+h} = (e^{(1)}_{t+h})^{2} - (e^{(2)}_{t+h})^{2}.
\end{equation}
The null hypothesis is that both models are equally accurate:
\begin{equation}
H_{0}:~ \mathbb{E}[d_{t+h}] = 0.
\end{equation}

The DM statistic is computed by standardizing the average loss differential:
\begin{equation}
\text{DM} = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{d}/N}},
\end{equation}

However, the original DM test is known to be oversized in small and moderate samples, particularly for longer horizons \parencite[p. 281-282]{Harvey1997}. Therefore, we use the modified DM test proposed by \textcite{Harvey1997}, known as the Harvey--Leybourne--Newbold (HLN) adjustment:
\begin{equation}
\text{DM}_{\text{HLN}} = \text{DM} \times 
\left( \frac{N + 1 - 2h + h(h/N)}{N} \right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}

A negative value of $\text{DM}_{\text{HLN}}$ indicates that model 1 yields lower average squared forecast errors than model 2. A statistically significant value of $\text{DM}_{\text{HLN}}$ implies that the difference in predictive accuracy between the two models is statistically significant.

\section{Economic Value Evaluation}

This section evaluates the economic usefulness of the yield forecasts in two realistic applications. First, we examine whether the predictions can improve the performance of a simple trading strategy based on expected yield movements. Second, we evaluate their usefulness in a risk-reduction simulation, assessing whether forecasts can reduce volatility and drawdowns.

\subsection{Long-Short Trading Strategy}

First, we evaluate a simple long--short trading strategy that takes directional investment
positions based on the sign of the forecasted excess return. The overall strategy
design and performance evaluation follow the approach outlined by
\textcite[pp.~2034--2035]{CarrieroKapetaniosMarcellino2012}. While a range of alternative
trading strategies exists, such as bond butterfly strategies
\parencite[pp.~14--17]{AudrinoSerwart2022}, the chosen approach has the advantage of being
simple and applicable to all models and maturities in a consistent manner.

Since investors respond to returns rather than raw yields, the model forecasts are
first transformed into holding-period returns.We approximate the holding-period return of a
bond with maturity $\tau$ over a horizon of $h$ trading days using a
duration-based approximation \parencite[p. 114]{Hull2018}. Specifically, the forecasted holding-period return between $t$ and $t+h$ is
given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:duration_return}
\hat{r}^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h}
\approx
- D_{\tau}
\left(
\hat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t+h|t} - y^{(\tau)}_{t}
\right),
\end{equation}
where $D_{\tau}$ denotes the modified duration associated with maturity $\tau$.

The corresponding forecasted excess return is obtained by subtracting the
short-term risk-free rate over the same horizon,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{er}^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h}
=
\hat{r}^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h}
-
y^{(1)}_{t+h} \cdot \frac{h}{252},
\end{equation}
where $y^{(1)}_{t}$ denotes the one-year Treasury yield at time $t$ and 252 denotes the trading days within a year. The realized excess
return is analogously defined using the realized holding period return.

Based on the sign of the forecasted excess return, a trading position is taken at
time $t$. Specifically, the strategy enters a long position in the bond if
$\widehat{er}^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h} > 0$ and a short position if
$\widehat{er}^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h} < 0$. The resulting trading profit realized at time
$t+h$ is therefore given by
\begin{equation}
\pi^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h}
=
\text{sign}\!\left(\widehat{er}^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h}\right)
\times er^{(\tau)}_{t,t+h}
\end{equation}

This mechanism can be illustrated by a simple example. Suppose the model predicts
that the yield on the 5-year Treasury will increase over the next five trading days.
Because bond prices move inversely to yields \parencite[p.~114]{Hull2018}, such an
increase implies a decline in the bond’s price over this period, resulting in a
negative holding-period return. After subtracting the short-term rate, the model
therefore issues a negative excess return forecast. In this case, the strategy
enters a short position in the 5-year bond, generating positive profits if yields
indeed rise as predicted.

The performance of the trading strategy is evaluated across all models, maturities,
and forecast horizons over the full out-of-sample period from January 2019 to
November 2025. The Random-Walk model is excluded from this comparison due to the lack of meaningful directional information in its forecasts. A investor cannot make a trading decision if all forecasts are identical to the last observed yield.
Performance is assessed using the Sharpe ratio as a measure of risk-adjusted returns
and the final wealth of an investor with an initial investment of 1{,}000. Following
\textcite[p.~2035]{CarrieroKapetaniosMarcellino2012}, the Sharpe ratio is computed as
\begin{equation}
\text{Sharpe}
=
\frac{\bar{\pi}}
{\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{t=1}^{N}
\left(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}\right)^2
}},
\end{equation}
where $\pi_t$ denotes the strategy return in period $t$ and $\bar{\pi}$ its sample
mean.

Final wealth is computed by assuming full reinvestment of gains and losses over
non-overlapping trading periods. Specifically, terminal wealth is given by
\begin{equation}
W_T
=
W_0
\prod_{t \in \mathcal{T}_h}
\left(
1
+
\text{sign}\!\left(\widehat{er}_{t,t+h}\right)
\cdot
er_{t,t+h}
\right),
\end{equation}
where $W_0 = 1{,}000$ denotes initial capital and $\mathcal{T}_h$ is the set of
non-overlapping forecast origin dates for horizon $h$. Ideally a good forecasting model produces high Sharpe ratios and high terminal wealth.

\subsection{Risk-Reduction Strategy}

To assess whether our models forecasts can be used to manage interest rate risk, we now evaluate a
risk-reduction strategy that controls bond market exposure based on yield forecasts. 
The key difference to the long-short trading strategy is that we're not trying to maximize returns through directional bets,
but rather reduce the overall risk of a bond portfolio by avoiding exposure during unfavorable market conditions.

The setup of the strategy works as follows. An investor holds a portfolio of U.S. Treasury bonds with 2-, 5-, and 10-year maturities.
Based on the model's yield forecasts, the investor decides at each rebalancing date whether to maintain full exposure to the bond portfolio or temporarily move to cash to reduce risk.
Decision are made on portfolio-level excess returns rather than individual bonds.
The benchmark for comparision is a buy-and-hold strategy that remains fully invested in the bond portfolio at all times.

At each forecast origin $t$, the model produces yield level forecasts
$\hat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t+h|t}$ for all maturities included in the portfolio. These
forecasts are mapped into predicted holding-period returns using the same
duration-based approximation we used in the trading strategy \eqref{eq:duration_return}.
The predicted portfolio return is obtained as the weighted average of the
maturity-specific predicted returns,
\begin{equation}
\hat{r}^{\text{port}}_{t,t+h}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{K} w_i \, \hat{r}^{(\tau_i)}_{t,t+h},
\end{equation}
and the corresponding forecasted excess return is given by
\begin{equation}
\widehat{er}^{\text{port}}_{t,t+h}
=
\hat{r}^{\text{port}}_{t,t+h}
-
y^{(1)}_{t+h} \cdot \frac{h}{252}.
\end{equation}

The forecasted portfolio excess return serves as a signal for the strategy. At each
rebalancing date $t$, the strategy takes bond market exposure only if the model
predicts a positive excess return,
\begin{equation}
\text{position}_t
=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } \widehat{er}^{\text{port}}_{t,t+h} > 0, \\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The realized payoff of the timing strategy at time $t+h$ is therefore given by
\begin{equation}
\pi^{\text{Timing}}_{t,t+h}
=
\text{position}_t
\times
er^{\text{port}}_{t,t+h}.
\end{equation}

where the realized portfolio excess calculated in the same way as the forecasted excess return, just using realized returns instead of forecasts.

Again, this can be illustrated with a simple example. Suppose the model predicts that yields for the 2- and 5-year bonds
will rise over the next month, while the 10-year yield is expected to decrease. The predicted holding-period returns for the 2- and 5-year bonds will be negative, while the 10-year bond's will be positive. If the weighted average of these predicted returns, after subtracting the risk-free rate, results in a negative forecasted excess return for the portfolio, the timing strategy will move to cash, thereby avoiding potential losses from rising yields.
If the yields move as predicted, the strategy has successfully reduced risk by avoiding exposure during unfavorable market conditions.

All evaluations are conducted over the out-of-sample period from
January 2019 to November 2025. Performance is assessed using maximum drawdown, volatility and final wealth. Additionally we will compute the difference of each metric compared to a naive buy-and-hold portfolio strategy to see if the forecasts allowed for a meaningful reduction in risk.
As in the trading strategy, the Random-Walk model is excluded from this comparision because of the lack of directional signals. Ideally a good forecast produces low volatility and low maximum drawdown while maintaining reasonable final wealth.

The observed RMSE patterns are consistent with previous findings in the interest rate forecasting literature. The strong performance of the Random Walk in short-horizon yield-curve forecasting is a result documented in numerous studies \parencite{CarrieroKapetaniosMarcellino2012,CaldeiraMouraSantos2016,diebold2006forecasting}. A reason for the similar performance of DNS and Ridge could be because both models rely on smooth, low-dimensional representations of the yield curve. While the DNS model was shown to be able to outperform the random-walk in longer forecasting horizons \parencite[p. 360]{diebold2006forecasting}, it doesn't seem to be able to do so in our shorter horizon setting. In contrast, XGBoost performs substantially worse, especially at longer horizons. This may stem from a couple of factors. First, the relatively small size of the dataset. \subsection{Diebold-Mariano Test Results}

The Diebold–Mariano test results reveal clear and statistically significant  differences in predictive accuracy across models, forecast horizons, and maturities. Across all maturities and horizons, the Random Walk significantly outperforms XGBoost, with p-values consistently below the 1 percent significance level. 
Comparisons between the Random Walk and the DNS model show a maturity-dependent pattern. While no statistically significant differences can be detected for the 2-year yield at any horizon, the Random Walk significantly outperforms DNS for the 5-year and 10-year maturities across all forecast horizons. 
Finally, the comparison with Ridge regression reveals a strong horizon-dependent effect. At short horizons of 1 and 5 days, the null hypothesis of equal predictive accuracy cannot be rejected for any maturity besides the 5-year, whereas at longer horizons of 10 and 30 days, the Random Walk exhibits statistically superior performance across all maturities.

Our observed patterns are in line with established interest rate theory. At short horizons, yield changes are largely unpredictable and closely resemble martingale differences \hl{insert source}, implying that structured models such as DNS and Ridge behave very similiar to the Random Walk, resulting in statistically equal forecast performance. 
At longer horizons, however, the accumulation of forecast and specification errors appears to outweigh any potential gains from the structure imposed by the models, leading to a significant decrease in the performance of DNS and Ridge relative to the Random Walk. 
The inferior and highly statistical signifcant performance of XGBoost further reinfoces our concerns mentioned in 5.1.2. The full set of Diebold--Mariano test results is reported in Table~\ref{tab:dm_full} in the Appendix.

Lastly, the final wealth outcomes are in line with the Sharpe ratio results, with DNS often leading to the highest terminal wealth. DNS, on the other hand, seems to better capture the directional dynamics of yields, leading to a translation of its forecast into economic gains. A possible explanation for this is that the RMSE treats positive and negative errors symmetrically, whereas in our trading setup the sign of the forecast is crucial. DNS's models structure (level, slope, curvature) seems to be better aligned with the directional movements, something the Ridge regression doesn't seems to capture as well.
XGBoost's poor performance in the trading strategy aligns with its high RMSE values, indicating that its forecasts lack both accuracy and directional usefulness compared to the other models.

We can conclude that statistical accuracy does not necessarily translate into economic value, as seen in the differing performance of DNS and Ridge. With regards to our research questions, we found no evidence that machine learning models provide additional economic value in our long-short trading strategy setup. Instead, traditional econometric models, in our case DNS, consistently delivers superior risk-adjusted returns. Due to the vast amount of different setups, strategies and model specifications used in the literature, a direct comparistion with previous studies is difficult. However, our findings are generally in line with the consensus that econometric models are difficult to beat \hl{insert source}.

\subsection{Risk-Reduction Strategy Results}

Table~\ref{tab:risk_reduction_delta} reports the performance of the risk-reduction strategy across models and forecast horizons. 
As mentioned in section 4.5.2 the RW is not included in this comparision. Several empirical observations emerge from the results.
First, all forecasting models outperform the Buy-and-Hold benchmark by a substantial margin. This improvement is reflected in consistently lower portfolio volatility, significantly reduced maximum drawdowns, and higher final wealth levels. In particular, the reduction in maximum drawdown ranges between approximately 8 and 19 percentage points, indicating economically meaningful risk reduction.
Second, no model clearly dominates the risk-reduction evaluation across all horizons. Ridge regression performs best at short horizons of one and five trading days, DNS exhibits the strongest performance at the ten-day horizon, and XGBoost achieves the largest drawdown reduction at the thirty-day horizon. Moreover, the differences between models are relatively small, especially at longer horizons, where all forecasts lead to very similar outcomes.

Figure~\ref{fig:risk_reduction_wealth} provides a visual illustration of the main findings of the risk-reduction analysis. The figure shows the evolution of portfolio wealth for the Buy-and-Hold benchmark and the timing strategies based on DNS, Ridge, and XGBoost forecasts over the complete OOS period. While the Buy-and-Hold strategy exhibits substantial drawdowns and significant wealth fluctuations, the timing strategies maintain a visibly more stable wealth path.
The period following 2022 is particularly informative. During the sharp increase in U.S. Treasury yields associated with the monetary tightening cycle in the beginning of 2022, the Buy-and-Hold portfolio experiences a pronounced decline in wealth. In contrast, the timing strategies are able to avoid a large fraction of this downside risk by exiting the bond market when forecasted excess returns turn negative. This behavior illustrates the economic value of yield curve forecasts for managing downside risk during periods of market stress. Also the similar performance of the models can be seen by the almost identical wealth curves of DNS, Ridge, and XGBoost.

These findings differ from the pattern observed in the long--short trading strategy and highlight an important distinction between directional trading and risk management. While the trading strategy requires accurate and stable forecasts of the direction of yield changes, the risk-reduction strategy primarily benefits from the ability to identify periods of elevated risk during which market exposure should be reduced.
From this perspective, machine learning models like Ridge and XGboost appear well-suited for the risk-reduction task. Ridge regression applies shrinkage to stabilize coefficients in the presence of multicollinearity and noise \parencite{hoerl1970ridge}. By supressing unstable directions, ridge regression effectively filters out short-term noise and focuses on persistent, systematic changes which is beneficial for distinguishing short-term fluctuations from more sustained risk patterns. XGBoost, in turn, builds predictions as an additive ensemble of decision trees, which allows it to capture non-linearities and treshold effects \parencite{chen2016xgboost}. This flexibility has been shown to be advantageous for detecting broader market regimes \parencite[p. 2]{delgado2022yieldcurve}, which helps explain its strong performance in the risk-reduction evaluation.

With regards to our research question, we can draw two conclusions. First, yield curve forecasts can provide substantial economic value in the context of risk-reduction by providing an investor with important information about when to avoid market exposure. Second, machine-learning models such as Ridge regression and XGBoost show promising results in detecting high-risk environments and can even beat econometric model in some scenarios.

\chapter{Robustness Check}
This chapter examines the robustness of our main findings, by employing a subsample analysis. We divide the OOS period into 2 equally sized intervals, with the first subsample spanning from January 2019 to June 2022 and the second from June 2022 to November 2025. We will re-evaluate the RMSE results as well as the risk-reduction strategy performance in both subsamples to see if our main findings hold across different market environments. The trading strategy is excluded from this robustness check because directional trading results are naturally very sensitive to specific market condiditions and the result of the subsamples would therefore be difficult to interpret. While it is beyond the scope of this thesis, to analyze which model performs best in which kind of macroeconomic regime, the subsample analysis still provides meaningfull information about generalizability of our findings. 

Looking at the RMSE robustness results, several intersting patterns emerge. For the second subsample, the model ranking closely resembles the findings from the full OOS period. In contrast, the first subsample exhibits a different performance pattern. Here, XGBoost unexpectedly outperforms all other models across a substantial number of maturities and forecast horizons. XGBoost is followed by the RW, while DNS and Ridge achieve a very similar and weaker performance.
This suggests that XGBoost was able to capture yield curve dynamics better than all other models during the first subsample, but that its forecasting performance deteriorated substantially in the second subsample. Moreover, the finding that DNS and Ridge perform very similarly and are consistently outperformed by the RW holds in both subsamples. This indicates that the underperformance of DNS and Ridge compared to the RW is robust across different market environments.

Looking at the risk-reduction robustness results, several similarities to the full-sample findings emerge. Across both subsamples, all forecasting models lead to substantial reductions in standard deviation and maximum drawdown relative to the buy-and-hold benchmark. No model consistently dominates the others across all horizons. Instead, the machine-learning models outperform the econometric benchmark in some cases, while being behind in others.
Importantly, these patterns are observed in both subsamples, indicating that the effectiveness of the risk-reduction strategy is not driven by a specific market regime.

In summary, our main findings regarding statistical forecast accuracy, in terms of RMSE, appear to be dependent on the market regime at that time. While XGBoost exhibits comparatively weak performance over the full OOS period, it outperforms the other models in the first half of the evaluation window. This indicates that the relative performance of more flexible machine-learning models can vary substantially across market environments.
In contrast, the results for the risk-reduction strategy appear to be more robust with respect to market conditions. Across both subsamples, the ranking of models remains consistent with the full OOS results, suggesting that these findings are less sensitive to regime changes. The full result tables of the robustness checks can be found in Table~\ref{tab:rmse_robustness_appendix} and ~\ref{tab:risk_reduction_robustness_compact} in the Appendix.

\chapter{Conclusion}
In this thesis, we had three main objectives. First, to develop a clean and reproducible forecasting framework and evaluation methodology that assesses both statistical accuracy and economic value. Second, to use this framework to examine whether machine-learning models such as ridge regression and XGBoost can improve short-horizon U.S. Treasury yield-curve forecasts relative to traditional econometric approaches. Third, to investigate whether gains in statistical forecast accuracy translate into economic value for an investor, measured through trading performance and risk-reduction outcomes.

With respect to the first objective, this thesis developed a transparent and reproducible framework for evaluating U.S. Treasury yield forecasts across different maturities and horizons. Statistical accuracy was assessed using RMSE and Diebold–Mariano tests, while economic value was evaluated through a long–short trading strategy and a risk-reduction simulation.
For the second objective, we find that no model consistently outperforms the random-walk benchmark in terms of statistical accuracy. Based on RMSE, a stable ranking was found with the random walk performing best, followed by DNS and Ridge regression with very similar accuracy, and XGBoost performing worst. While the differences between DNS, Ridge, and the random walk are not statistically significant in several cases, XGBoost is consistently and significantly outperformed by the benchmark.
Regarding the third objective, the results show that statistical accuracy does not directly translate into economic value. In the trading strategy, the DNS model substantially outperforms Ridge regression despite comparable RMSE values. Overall, DNS delivers the strongest trading performance, followed by Ridge regression and XGBoost. In contrast, the risk-reduction exercise yields more uniform results: all models achieve substantial reductions in volatility and drawdowns relative to a buy-and-hold benchmark, with no model clearly dominating across all horizons.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this thesis. First, the results for statistical forecast accuracy are not fully robust across different market regimes. While the risk-reduction results remain broadly stable across subperiods, RMSE rankings vary over time, indicating that forecast accuracy may be sensitive to market conditions.
Second, the empirical analysis is based on a relatively limited dataset. Incorporating more historical samples, additional maturities, or longer forecasting horizons could provide a more comprehensive assessment.
Third, the forecasting models employed in this thesis are kept relatively simple. More complex specifications such as adding macroeconomic variables, richer feature engineering, or more extensive hyperparameter tuning could potentially improve performance and deliver additional insights.

Future research could extend this analysis in several directions. One extension would be to incorporate macroeconomic variables such as inflation, output growth, or monetary policy indicators to our framework, as already used in \textcite{diebold2006macroeconomy} or \textcite{bie2024machine}. Additionally, other machine-learning models could be explored. In particular, more flexible architectures such as neural networks or transformer models, which have gained increasing attention in recent years, may be interesting to use, as \parencite{Wang2025} and \parencite{das2025yieldcurve} show.

In conclusion, the opening quote that “all models are wrong, but some are useful” is well reflected in the findings of this thesis. While machine-learning models do not consistently outperform traditional econometric approaches in terms of statistical accuracy, they nevertheless prove useful by generating economic value, particularly in risk-reduction applications.
